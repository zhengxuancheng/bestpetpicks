Every time I let the dog out, I stop what I'm doing, get up, walk to the back door, and stand there for a minute while she sniffs the fence line like she's checking her email. Multiply that by six or seven trips a day and it adds up to a real chunk of lost focus. A pet door fixes that. You install it once, and your dog or cat decides when they need out. You stay put.

The catch is picking the right one. Pet doors come in four basic styles: in-door flaps you cut into a solid door, wall-mounted units with a tunnel, sliding glass inserts that need no drilling, and electronic or smart doors that only open for your pet's collar key. Each solves a different problem. I tested six of the best across all four styles, from a budget flap under $50 to a smart door with its own app, and broke down what actually matters before you buy.

## What to Look For in a Pet Door

### Where You'll Mount It

This decides everything else. If you have a solid wood or PVC door, a standard flap door is the cheapest route, but you're cutting a hole in that door, so measure twice. Renters usually go for a sliding glass insert, which sits in the existing track and needs zero drilling. Wall-mounted doors with a tunnel are the most permanent option and the best fit when the door is too narrow or the pet needs to pass through a thick wall. Screen doors need a lightweight flap made for thin frames. Decide the location first, then shop.

### Flap Size: Measure the Pet, Not the Door

The most common mistake is buying on the pet's weight instead of their chest. A Labrador and a Greyhound can weigh the same and need very different flaps, because the flap has to clear the widest part of the chest and the shoulders. Measure your pet standing, chest width across the widest point, then add an inch or two. The flap opening should be at least as wide as that. A dog that has to squeeze through a too-small flap will stop using the door and start telling you at 6am instead.

### Insulation and Energy Loss

A pet door is a hole in your house, and heat leaves through it. Quality doors fight this with double flaps, magnetic seals, and weather stripping around the frame. The best insulated doors keep the flap sealed when the pet isn't pushing through, which matters in winter and in air-conditioned summers. If you live somewhere with real seasons, don't cheap out here. The energy savings from a well-sealed door can pay back the price difference within a year or two.

### Security: Strays and Raccoons

A simple flap lets anything in that can push it. If you have raccoons, possums, or neighbor cats in your area, look for a door with a lock-out panel, a lockable flap, or an electronic key. Electronic doors read a chip or key on the pet's collar and only open for that pet, which completely solves the stray problem. Smart doors add schedules, so the flap locks itself at night or while you're away. If strays are a real issue in your neighborhood, an electronic door is worth the extra money.

### Durability

The flap gets pushed, clawed, and chewed daily, and it sits outside in sun, rain, and freezing temperatures. Look for aluminum frames over plastic, and UV-resistant flaps that stay flexible in the cold. A cheap flap that stiffens at 20°F will eventually crack and let drafts through. The best doors in this roundup are built to outlast the pet that uses them.

## FAQ

### How do I measure my dog for a pet door?

Measure the widest point of your dog's chest while they're standing, then add 1 to 2 inches. That's the minimum flap width you need. Don't rely on weight alone: a deep-chested breed needs a wider flap than a slim dog of the same weight. When in doubt, size up. A door that's slightly too big still seals fine, but one that's too small will get ignored.

### Can I install a pet door in a sliding glass door without drilling?

Yes. Sliding glass inserts like the Ideal Pet Products Fast Fit sit in the door track and expand to fit your opening. You close the sliding door against the panel and it stays put with no holes drilled. It's the standard choice for renters, and it comes out cleanly when you move.

### Will a pet door let other animals in?

A basic flap will. If strays, raccoons, or possums visit your yard, get a door with a lock-out panel you can close at night, or go electronic. Electronic doors read a key or chip on your pet's collar and won't open for anything else. That's the only way to be fully sure no uninvited guests come through.

### Do pet doors waste a lot of energy?

Some do, but the good ones don't. Look for double flaps, magnetic seals, and weather stripping around the frame. Doors like the Endura Flap are designed to stay sealed when nothing is pushing through, so the heat loss is small. In extreme climates, an electronic door that locks at night adds another layer of protection.

### Electronic or manual pet door: which should I get?

If you just need your pet to come and go, a manual door is cheaper, simpler, and has nothing to break. If you have stray animals, want a schedule, or like the idea of locking the door from your phone, go electronic. The collar-key doors are the middle ground: selective entry without an app or a subscription.
