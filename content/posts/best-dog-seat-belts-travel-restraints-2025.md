A dog bouncing around your car is a distraction. A 50-pound dog in a sudden stop becomes a 2,000-pound projectile. That is why every safety organization, from the Center for Pet Safety to the ASPCA, says you need to restrain your dog in the car.

But finding a restraint that actually works is harder than it should be. Some clip to collars (neck injury risk). Some have buckles that pop open on impact. Some are not crash-tested at all. We tested six restraints with a range of dogs and driving conditions to find the ones that actually keep your dog safe.

## What to Look For in a Dog Seat Belt

**Crash testing certification.** This is the most important factor. A "crash-tested" label means the manufacturer paid an independent lab to test the restraint at real crash speeds. Look for certification from the Center for Pet Safety (CPS) or compliance with FMVSS (Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards). Without it, you are trusting a marketing claim.

**Harness vs. tether.** Some restraints are full harnesses that wrap around your dog's body. Others are short tethers that clip from your dog's harness to the car's seat belt buckle. Harnesses generally offer better crash protection because they distribute impact forces across the chest and shoulders rather than pulling on one point.

**Attachment method.** Seat belt buckle attachments are the most secure. Clip-on attachments that loop around the headrest are convenient but less tested in crash scenarios. LATCH-compatible restraints offer another secure option.

**Comfort and fit.** A harness your dog hates will end up in the closet. Look for padded chest plates, adjustable straps, and materials that do not chafe. Measure your dog's chest girth and neck before buying.

## FAQ

### Is a dog seat belt safe enough in a crash?

It depends on the product and how you use it. A crash-tested harness like the Sleepypod Clickit Sport provides real protection at highway speeds. A basic tether clipped to a flimsy harness will not hold up in a serious collision. Buy a restraint that has been independently tested. Do not buy one just because it looks sturdy.

### Can I use a dog seat belt with a collar?

No. Never attach a seat belt tether to a collar. In a crash, the collar can choke your dog or cause neck and spine injuries. Always clip to a harness that distributes force across the chest and shoulders.

### How do I install a dog seat belt harness?

It varies by product. Most harnesses wrap around your dog's body and clip into the car's seat belt buckle. Some route the car's belt through a channel on the harness. Read the manual. The Sleepypod Clickit has a multi-step process that involves threading the car belt behind the harness flaps and buckling normally.

### Should I get a harness or a tether?

A harness. A full crash-tested harness distributes impact forces across the chest. A tether relies entirely on the harness you pair it with and adds no protection of its own. If you already have a sturdy walking harness, a tether is a reasonable addition. If you are starting from nothing, buy a crash-tested harness.

### Are dog seat belts required by law?

Several states have laws requiring dogs to be restrained in moving vehicles. New Jersey, Hawaii, and Maine have the strictest laws. Other states consider an unrestrained dog a distracted driving hazard. Even where not required, restraining your dog is safer for everyone in the car.
