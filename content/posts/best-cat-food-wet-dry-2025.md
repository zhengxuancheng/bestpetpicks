## What to Look For in Cat Food

### Protein Source and Quality

Cats are obligate carnivores. They need animal protein, not plant protein. Look for a named protein source as the first ingredient — chicken, turkey, salmon, or beef. Avoid foods where the first ingredient is corn, wheat, or a vague by-product meal. The Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) sets minimum protein levels, but most cats do better on food with 35-45% protein on a dry matter basis. Senior cats and cats with kidney issues may need reduced protein, so check with your vet.

### Moisture Content

Dry food contains 6-10% moisture. Wet food contains 75-85% moisture. This matters for two reasons. Cats have a low thirst drive and evolved to get most of their water from prey. A cat eating only dry food often lives in a state of mild chronic dehydration, which strains the kidneys and urinary tract over time. Wet food provides hydration directly. Many vets recommend feeding at least some wet food, especially for male cats prone to urinary blockages. Even adding one can of wet food per day makes a meaningful difference.

### Carbohydrates

Cats have no dietary requirement for carbohydrates. Their bodies are designed to convert protein and fat into energy. But most dry cat foods are 20-40% carbs because starch is needed to bind kibble together. Lower-carb foods (under 15% calories from carbs) are closer to a cat's natural diet and may help with weight management and blood sugar regulation. Grain-free does not automatically mean low-carb — pea starch and potato starch are still carbs.

### Calories and Feeding Guidelines

A 10-pound cat needs about 200-250 calories per day. Check the calorie content per cup or can, then compare to the feeding guidelines. The guidelines on the bag are usually generous (they want you to use more food). Start with the lower end of the range and adjust based on whether your cat is maintaining, gaining, or losing weight. An indoor cat with a desk job needs fewer calories than a barn cat that spends all day hunting.

### Life Stage Appropriateness

Kitten food has more protein, fat, and calcium for growth. Adult food maintains. Senior food may have joint supplements, lower phosphorus for kidneys, and adjusted protein. Most foods labeled "all life stages" meet kitten nutritional requirements, which means they are calorie-dense and may cause weight gain in less active adult cats. Feed the right life stage for your cat.

## FAQ

### How much should I feed my cat per day?

A 10-pound cat needs roughly 200-250 calories per day. Check the calorie content on your food's packaging. For dry food, that is typically 1/3 to 1/2 cup per day split into two meals. For wet food, 2-3 cans per day depending on can size. Adjust based on your cat's activity level and whether they are gaining or losing weight. Most cats do well on measured portions rather than free-feeding.

### Is grain-free cat food better?

Not inherently. Grain-free food replaces grains (corn, wheat, rice) with alternative carbohydrate sources like peas, potatoes, or lentils. That usually means lower carbs and higher protein, which is closer to a cat's natural diet. But some grain-free foods are still high in carbs from starches. Check the actual carb content rather than relying on the grain-free label. There is no evidence that grains are harmful to cats unless they have a specific allergy.

### Should I feed wet food, dry food, or both?

Both is the best answer for most cats. Wet food provides hydration. Dry food provides convenience and dental benefits (the crunch can help reduce plaque). A combination of at least one wet meal per day plus free-choice dry food works well for most households. Cats with urinary issues or kidney disease especially benefit from wet food's moisture content.

### How do I switch my cat to a new food?

Transition gradually over 7-10 days. Start with 25% new food mixed with 75% old food for 2-3 days. Move to 50/50 for another 2-3 days. Go to 75/25 for 2-3 days. Then fully switch. Sudden food changes cause vomiting and diarrhea in most cats. If your cat refuses the new food, try mixing in a small amount of warm water or topper to increase the smell appeal.

### What ingredients should I avoid in cat food?

Avoid vague by-products where the source animal is not named. Avoid artificial colors, flavors, and preservatives like BHA, BHT, and ethoxyquin. Avoid foods with added sugar or corn syrup. Avoid foods where the first three ingredients are all grains or plant proteins rather than animal proteins. Watch out for high carbohydrate content in foods labeled "grain-free" — check the guaranteed analysis and call the manufacturer for carb percentages.
