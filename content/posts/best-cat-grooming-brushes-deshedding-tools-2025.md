Some cats shed enough fur each spring to build a second cat. Others get mats in their armpits no matter how often you brush them. And some just hate being brushed full stop. Over the years I've gone through a lot of cat brushes — the cheap ones that barely catch loose hair, the aggressive ones that make your cat flinch, and a few that actually do what they're supposed to.

A good deshedding tool will cut loose undercoat by 80-90%, and the right slicker brush keeps long hair cats mat-free without turning grooming into a wrestling match. I tested six options across different price points and cat temperaments.

## What to Look For in a Cat Grooming Brush

### Coat Type Matters Most

Your cat tells you what brush you need. Short hair cats need something that catches loose undercoat without irritating the skin on top — a fine-tooth deshedding tool or a rubber curry brush works great. Long hair cats (Persians, Maine Coons, Ragdolls) need deeper penetration to prevent mats, which means a wide-tooth comb or a slicker brush with long, rounded pins. Double-coated breeds need a deshedding tool that reaches the undercoat without cutting the topcoat.

### Blade vs. Pin vs. Bristle

Three main types of brush heads exist. Deshedding blades have stainless steel teeth that pull loose undercoat from deep in your cat's fur — they do the heavy lifting. Slicker brushes have fine wire pins set in a cushioned pad, good for detangling and smoothing. Bristle brushes work on short hair cats and distribute natural oils across the coat for shine. Most thorough grooming sessions use two or three brush types.

### Comfort for You and the Cat

A brush that hurts your hand after five minutes won't get used. Ergonomic handles with rubber grips make a real difference. Equally important: the brush should not hurt the cat. Rounded pin tips (not sharp ones) and pressure-sensitive heads prevent scratching. If your cat tolerates the brush, you'll brush more often — and that's what prevents shedding on furniture and painful mats.

### Easy to Clean

A brush that traps hair and won't let go is frustrating. Self-cleaning slicker brushes have a button that retracts the pins, letting you lift off the hair clump in one piece. Deshedding tools usually have a finger groove that lets you push hair off the blade. If cleaning the brush is a chore, you'll use it less.

### Replacement Blades

Deshedding tools dull over time. Brands like FURminator sell replacement blades, which extends the life of the tool considerably. A dull deshedder pulls and snags instead of cutting loose hair — which is uncomfortable for the cat and less effective for you.

## FAQ

### How often should I brush my cat?

Short hair cats need brushing 1-2 times per week. Long hair cats need brushing 3-4 times per week, ideally daily during shedding seasons (spring and fall). Cats that don't get brushed regularly are more likely to develop mats and hairballs from swallowing loose fur.

### Does brushing reduce hairballs?

Yes. Hairballs form when cats swallow loose fur during self-grooming. Brushing removes that loose fur before your cat can swallow it. I notice that when I brush my own cats daily during shedding season, the hairballs go from a weekly problem to rare.

### What's the difference between a deshedding tool and a regular brush?

A deshedding tool has a stainless steel edge that cuts through the topcoat to reach loose undercoat hair. A regular brush only catches surface hair and loose topcoat. For cats that shed heavily, a deshedding tool removes several times more loose fur than a regular brush.

### Can I use a dog brush on my cat?

You can, but it's not ideal. Cat skin is thinner and more sensitive than dog skin. Dog brushes designed for thicker coats may be too aggressive for cats. It's better to use brushes specifically designed for feline coats.

### My cat hates being brushed. What do I do?

Start slowly. Let the cat sniff the brush. Brush for just 10-15 seconds at a time and give treats afterward. Focus on areas the cat enjoys being petted (cheeks, chin, back) and avoid sensitive spots (belly, tail, legs) until trust builds. Some cats that hate slicker brushes tolerate rubber curry brushes or grooming gloves.
